Every bee
has a cell.

A tale about a hive that learned to work as one —
and what happened when trouble came knocking.

The meadow grew scattered.

Every garden raised its own hive — its own dance, its own doors, its own rules. A bee from one hive couldn't so much as ask another for directions.

Whole fields of nectar sat unclaimed — not because no one wanted it, but because no one could ask for it.

Then came a common dance.

One master bee. One signal, sung in the open — and every bee that hears it understands, no matter which garden raised it.

The master never grabs a bee and drags it. It asks, once, in a language all of them share — and exactly the right bees answer. One voice, many listeners, zero translators.

The bees do the work — at the source.

Out to the blossoms, right where the good stuff is made. The courier drinks straight from the flower: no queue at someone else's hive, no stranger holding the droplet along the way.

What it gathers, it keeps whole. Nothing skimmed, nothing lost, no toll booths between the flower and home.

Then trouble found the hive.

A black bumblebee — twice the size, all muscle, no manners — dropped out of the sky, straight for the comb.

It hit a wall no one had ever seen. The shield was on before the hive knew it was needed. The bumblebee bounced. The bee next door never even looked up.

Every drop comes home, counted.

Each courier docks at its own cell — and only its own cell opens. The droplets flow to the heart of the hive, where every drop is weighed in front of everyone.

The credit goes to the exact bee that earned it — written into a ledger the whole meadow can read, and no one can rewrite.

We're building this hive.

A comb where every member has its own sealed cell. One common dance instead of a hundred dialects. Work done at the source and carried home whole. A shield that's simply always on. Every drop counted, and credited to whoever earned it.
